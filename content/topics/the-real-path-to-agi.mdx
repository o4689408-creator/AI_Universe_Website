Few terms in AI generate more disagreement than "AGI" — artificial general intelligence. Ask ten researchers what it means and how close we are, and expect ten different answers. That disagreement is itself informative.

## What "general" is actually asking for

Today's most capable models are remarkably good at a wide range of tasks, but they remain narrower than the term "general intelligence" implies — strong in pattern-matching over vast training data, weaker at the kind of reliable, transferable reasoning a person does without a script.

## Why definitions matter more than they seem to

Different definitions of AGI imply completely different timelines and different things to measure. A definition centered on economic task-replacement produces a very different estimate than one centered on autonomous, open-ended reasoning across genuinely novel situations.

<Callout title="Go deeper: capability vs. autonomy">

Many measures of "AGI progress" focus on capability — can a model solve a given benchmark task? A smaller but arguably more important question is autonomy — can a system pursue a multi-step goal, correct its own course when it fails, and operate reliably without a human checking each step? Current systems have made faster progress on the former than the latter.

</Callout>

<PullQuote>
The gap between "impressive on a benchmark" and "reliable in the real world" is where most AGI predictions quietly break down.
</PullQuote>

## What's actually driving progress right now

Scale, better training techniques, and increasingly sophisticated post-training (teaching a model to reason step by step, use tools, and check its own work) have driven most of the visible progress in the last several years — not a single new breakthrough architecture.

## Why healthy skepticism is warranted — in both directions

It's just as easy to dismiss AGI as science fiction as it is to assume it's imminent. The most defensible position sits between the two: the trajectory of capability has been genuinely fast and worth taking seriously, while specific timeline predictions have a poor track record precisely because the definition keeps moving.

<DidYouKnow>

The term "artificial general intelligence" only became widely used in AI research in the early 2000s — decades after "artificial intelligence" itself. Earlier AI researchers largely assumed general intelligence was simply what AI would eventually mean, until narrow, task-specific systems became the field's dominant success story and a separate term was needed for the original, broader goal.

</DidYouKnow>

## Key takeaways

<KeyTakeaways>

- "AGI" means different things to different researchers, and the definition used quietly determines the timeline estimate.
- Capability and autonomy are progressing at different rates.
- Recent progress has come mostly from scale and post-training techniques, not a single new breakthrough.
- The most defensible stance takes the trajectory seriously while remaining skeptical of specific timelines.

</KeyTakeaways>
